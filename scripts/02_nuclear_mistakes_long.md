## FILE 1 — THE GLOWING GIRLS (1917)
New Jersey, 1917.

America has a new miracle product: radium. It glows in the dark. People put it in drinks and face creams. It's sold as health.

At a factory in Orange, New Jersey, hundreds of young women get one of the best-paying jobs in town: painting glow-in-the-dark numbers on watch dials.

The brushes are tiny. To keep a sharp tip, they're taught a trick. Lip, dip, paint. Put the brush between your lips, dip it in radium paint, and paint. Hundreds of times a day.

The women glow. Literally. Their hair, their clothes, their skin shine in the dark. Some even paint their teeth for fun before a night out.

Then they start getting sick.

Teeth fall out. Jaws crumble. Bones break on their own. Doctors call it "radium jaw."

The company's answer? It's not the radium.

In 1927, five dying women, the newspapers called them the Radium Girls, took the company to court. In 1928, they won.

Their fight helped create the worker safety laws we still rely on today.

And their bodies? They're still radioactive. Radium takes 1,600 years to lose just half of its radioactivity.

But radium was only the beginning. Twenty years later, scientists were playing with something far more dangerous.

## FILE 2 — THE DEMON CORE (1945)
Los Alamos, New Mexico. August 1945.

The war is over. But there's one bomb core left. A ball of plutonium, about the size of a grapefruit. It was meant for a third atomic bomb. Now, scientists use it for experiments.

The experiment is simple, and insanely dangerous. They surround the core with material that reflects neutrons back into it, bringing it right to the edge of a chain reaction. Close… but not over. They called it "tickling the dragon's tail."

August 21st. Harry Daghlian is working late, alone except for a security guard. He's stacking heavy blocks around the core by hand. One slips from his fingers and falls next to the core.

The ball goes critical. He pulls the block away, but it's too late. Harry Daghlian dies 25 days later.

Nine months later, it happens again.

Louis Slotin is showing colleagues the same kind of test. He holds a metal half-shell over the core. The only thing keeping it from closing? A flathead screwdriver.

The screwdriver slips.

A blue flash fills the room. Slotin feels a wave of heat. He jerks the top half off with his hand, stopping the reaction, and probably saving the seven other people in the room.

He dies nine days later.

After that, the scientists gave the core a new name. The Demon Core.

## FILE 3 — THE BOMB WE LOST (1958)
February 5th, 1958. 2 a.m. Over the coast of Georgia.

A B-47 bomber is flying a practice mission. It's carrying a Mark 15 nuclear bomb. Over 3,000 kilograms.

Suddenly, an F-86 fighter jet slams into its wing.

The fighter pilot ejects. The bomber is badly damaged, and its pilot, Major Howard Richardson, has a choice. Try to land with the bomb, and risk it exploding on the runway… or drop it.

He drops it into the water near Tybee Island, close to the city of Savannah.

Then he lands the broken plane.

The Air Force searches for nine weeks. Divers. Sonar. Around a hundred men.

They never find it.

To this day, the Tybee bomb is somewhere in the mud off the Georgia coast.

The Air Force says the nuclear capsule wasn't inside, so it can't cause a nuclear blast. But in 1966, a Pentagon official described it to Congress as a "complete weapon."

Officially, it's not dangerous. Unofficially… nobody can check.

And three years later, another bomber dropped not one, but two bombs. This time, on land.

## FILE 4 — ONE SWITCH (1961)
Remember that bomber over North Carolina?

January 24th, 1961. A B-52 develops a fuel leak near Goldsboro. The plane starts breaking apart in the air. The crew bails out. Three of them die.

Two Mark 39 hydrogen bombs fall from the wreck.

The first bomb's parachute opens. It drifts down and lands in a field, the parachute caught in a tree. When soldiers reach it, they find something terrifying. As it fell, the bomb went through the steps of arming itself. Three of its four safety mechanisms had already given way.

The only one left was a single switch, controlled from the cockpit… still set to "safe."

For decades, the public was told there was never any real danger. Then, in 2013, a secret report was released. An engineer who studied the bomb wrote that "one simple, dynamo-technology, low-voltage switch stood between the United States and a major catastrophe."

And the second bomb? Its parachute never opened. It hit a muddy field at over 1,000 kilometers per hour and broke apart. Part of it sank so deep that it was never recovered.

It's still down there. The government bought rights to the land, so nobody can dig.

But all of these were accidents. The next one wasn't. It was a choice. And one man had to make it.

## FILE 5 — THE MAN WHO SAVED THE WORLD (1983)
September 26th, 1983. Just after midnight. A secret bunker outside Moscow.

The Cold War is at its worst. Three weeks earlier, the Soviet Union shot down a Korean passenger jet, killing all 269 people on board. Both sides are on edge.

Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov is on duty, watching the Soviet satellite warning system.

Suddenly, the alarm goes off. The screen flashes one word. LAUNCH.

An American missile is heading toward the Soviet Union.

Then a second. A third. Five missiles.

Petrov's job is to report it up the chain. If he does, Soviet leaders could have only minutes to decide whether to strike back. With hundreds of nuclear missiles.

But something doesn't feel right.

Why would America start a nuclear war with only five missiles? A real attack would be hundreds. The system is new. And ground radar shows nothing.

He has no way to be sure. He has to trust his gut.

He picks up the phone… and reports it as a false alarm.

Then he waits. If he's wrong, warheads will start landing within minutes.

Nothing lands.

He was right. The satellites had mistaken sunlight reflecting off high clouds for missile launches.

Petrov got no medal. The story stayed secret for 15 years. And when he died in 2017, the world didn't even find out for months.

The man who may have saved the world died almost unknown.

## BONUS FILE — EARTH DID IT FIRST
But the strangest nuclear story of all? No human made a mistake.

In 1972, at a French nuclear facility, workers checking uranium noticed something impossible. Uranium from one mine in Gabon, Africa, had less of the reactive kind than uranium anywhere else on Earth. About 200 kilograms of it seemed to be missing.

Was it stolen? Was someone building a bomb?

No. The answer was two billion years old.

Back then, natural uranium was much richer. At Oklo, groundwater seeped into the uranium deposit, and a chain reaction started. On its own.

Earth had built a natural nuclear reactor. It ran for hundreds of thousands of years. About 30 minutes on, two and a half hours off, as the water boiled away and came back.

So the first nuclear reactor on Earth wasn't built by humans.

We just showed up two billion years late.
