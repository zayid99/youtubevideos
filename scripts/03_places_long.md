## FILE 1 — THE LAND NOBODY WANTS (Bir Tawil)
File one. The Land Nobody Wants.

On the border between Egypt and Sudan, there's a piece of desert called Bir Tawil. It's 2,060 square kilometers. Almost three times the size of Singapore.

And not a single country on Earth claims it.

Here's how that happened. In 1899, Britain drew a straight border between Egypt and Sudan, right along the 22nd parallel. Three years later, it drew a second line, to match where local tribes actually lived.

The two lines don't agree. And between them are two pieces of land.

One is the Hala'ib Triangle. More than 20,000 square kilometers, with towns, people, and a coast on the Red Sea.

The other is Bir Tawil. Rock, sand, and no water.

Egypt says the first line is the real border. That gives Egypt the Hala'ib Triangle. Sudan says the second line is the real one. That gives Sudan the Hala'ib Triangle.

But here's the catch. On Egypt's map, Bir Tawil belongs to Sudan. On Sudan's map, it belongs to Egypt. Claiming it would mean accepting the other side's border, and giving up the far more valuable land.

So both countries point at Bir Tawil and say the same thing. It's yours.

Of course, people noticed. In 2014, a farmer from Virginia named Jeremiah Heaton traveled across the desert and planted a flag there on his daughter's seventh birthday. He called it the Kingdom of North Sudan, so she could be a real princess.

No country ever recognized it.

Today, Bir Tawil is still one of the only places on Earth that nobody owns.

But if a place nobody wants sounds strange, wait until you see a place two countries share. Six months at a time.

## FILE 2 — THE ISLAND THAT CHANGES COUNTRIES (Pheasant Island)
File two. The Island That Changes Countries.

On the border between France and Spain, in the middle of the Bidasoa River, there's a tiny island. About 200 meters long, and 40 meters wide.

It's called Pheasant Island.

In 1659, after twenty-four years of war, France and Spain needed somewhere to make peace. So they met right here, in the middle of the river, where neither side had to set foot on the other's land.

The result was the Treaty of the Pyrenees. And part of the deal was this island. They decided to share it.

Not by splitting it in half. By splitting the year.

From February 1st to July 31st, Pheasant Island is Spanish. From August 1st to January 31st, it's French.

Each country even names a viceroy to run it: a naval commander from each side. Probably the smallest territory any viceroy has ever ruled.

A year after the treaty, the island hosted a royal handover. The kings of France and Spain met here, and the Spanish princess Maria Theresa crossed over to marry Louis the Fourteenth.

Today, nobody lives on Pheasant Island. The public can only visit on rare guided tours.

And every six months, it quietly changes country. It has been doing it for more than 350 years.

## FILE 3 — TOMORROW AND YESTERDAY (Diomede Islands)
File three. Tomorrow and Yesterday.

In the Bering Strait, between Alaska and Russia, there are two rocky islands. Big Diomede belongs to Russia. Little Diomede belongs to the United States.

They're less than four kilometers apart. On a clear day, you can see one from the other.

But the International Date Line runs right between them. So Big Diomede is about 21 hours ahead. When it's Monday morning on Little Diomede, it's already Tuesday on Big Diomede.

That's why people call them Tomorrow Island and Yesterday Island.

For centuries, the same Indigenous families lived on both islands and traveled freely between them. Then came the Cold War. The border closed. The people of Big Diomede were moved to the Russian mainland, and the island became a military zone. This tiny stretch of water became part of what people called the Ice Curtain.

In winter, the sea between them freezes solid. You could almost walk from America to Russia. It's just not allowed.

Then, in 1987, an American swimmer named Lynne Cox decided to cross it anyway.

She dove into the Bering Strait at Little Diomede. The water was just a few degrees above freezing. She swam for more than two hours, crossed the Date Line, and climbed out on Soviet soil. Into tomorrow.

Four months later, at the White House, Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev raised a toast. He said it had taken one American just two hours to swim from one of their countries to the other.

Two superpowers, closer than they thought.

And speaking of things drifting apart, there's a place in Africa where the ground itself is tearing open.

## FILE 4 — WHERE AFRICA IS TEARING APART (Afar)
File four. Where Africa Is Tearing Apart.

September 2005. The Afar region of Ethiopia. One of the hottest, lowest, and harshest places on Earth.

It started with earthquakes. Hundreds of them. Then a volcano erupted. And over just a few days, the ground split open.

A crack about 60 kilometers long tore through the desert. In places, it was 6 meters wide. Wide enough to swallow a bus.

When scientists measured it, they were stunned. In just a few weeks, the two sides had moved apart by up to 8 meters. Movement that normally takes centuries.

Here's why. Afar sits right where three of Earth's plates meet: the Arabian plate, the Nubian plate, and the Somali plate. And they're all pulling away from each other.

Most of the time, it happens slowly. Just a few millimeters a year. Slower than your fingernails grow.

But over millions of years, those millimeters add up. Scientists think the Horn of Africa will eventually break away. The sea will flood in. And a brand new ocean will be born, right where people live today.

We're watching a continent split in real time. We just won't be around for the ending.

And if you want to see real isolation, the next place is so remote that the closest humans aren't even on Earth.

## FILE 5 — THE LONELIEST PLACE ON EARTH (Point Nemo)
File five. The Loneliest Place on Earth.

Somewhere in the South Pacific, there's a spot farther from land than anywhere else on the planet.

2,688 kilometers from the nearest shore, in every direction. And that nearest land? Three tiny islands where nobody lives.

For centuries, nobody knew exactly where this point was. Then, in 1992, a survey engineer named Hrvoje Lukatela found it with a computer program.

It's called Point Nemo, after Captain Nemo from Jules Verne's novel. In Latin, Nemo means "no one."

And here's the strangest part. The International Space Station orbits about 400 kilometers above Earth. So when it passes over Point Nemo, the closest humans to that spot are the astronauts in space.

Because it's so empty, space agencies use this part of the ocean as a graveyard.

When old satellites and cargo ships come back to Earth, they're steered down here, where they can't hit anyone. Hundreds of spacecraft are believed to lie on the ocean floor in this region. Including Mir, the Soviet-era space station, which came down in 2001.

And one day, the International Space Station itself is planned to end its life in this same lonely stretch of ocean.

Even the sounds here are strange. In 1997, underwater microphones picked up an enormous, unexplained noise from this remote part of the Pacific. Scientists called it the Bloop. For years, people imagined giant sea creatures. The real answer was probably an icequake. A massive iceberg, cracking apart.

The loneliest place on Earth. And it's full of spaceships.

## BONUS FILE — THE TOWN WHERE YOUR DOOR DECIDES YOUR COUNTRY (Baarle)
Bonus file. The Town Where Your Front Door Decides Your Country.

On the border between Belgium and the Netherlands, there's a town called Baarle. Or really, two towns: Baarle-Hertog, which is Belgian, and Baarle-Nassau, which is Dutch.

Their border isn't one line. It's a patchwork. Thanks to land deals going back to the Middle Ages, there are 22 little pieces of Belgium scattered inside the Netherlands. And inside some of those pieces of Belgium, there are 7 pieces of the Netherlands.

The border runs through streets, shops, gardens, and even houses. It's marked right on the ground with white crosses.

So which country do you live in? Simple. Wherever your front door is.

House numbers even carry a little flag, to show which country you're in. Some owners have moved their front door to change countries. And on one street, the border runs straight through a front door.

In some homes, you can cross an international border on your way to the kitchen.
